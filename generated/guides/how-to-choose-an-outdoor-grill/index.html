How to Choose an Outdoor Grill (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Choosing a grill starts with a fuel-type decision that shapes everything about how you'll cook — and there's no right answer, only the right fit for how you like to grill. After that, the spec that gets shouted (BTUs) matters less than the one that doesn't (build material). Here's how to pick a grill you'll still love in five summers.

Fuel type is the real decision

Gas (propane or natural gas) is about convenience — instant heat, precise control, ready in minutes, easy cleanup. Charcoal delivers that classic smoky flavor and high searing heat, but takes time to light and more effort to manage and clean. Pellet grills run on wood pellets with electronic temperature control, excelling at low-and-slow smoking and set-and-forget cooking, though they sear less aggressively and need power.

Kamado (ceramic) grills hold heat for hours and do everything from searing to smoking to baking. Decide what matters most — speed, flavor, or smoking ability — because it rules out whole sections of the market.

Why BTUs mislead

Gas grills advertise total BTUs, and buyers assume more equals hotter and better. Not really — a high BTU number spread across a huge, poorly insulated grill can run cooler than a modest BTU figure in a well-built, properly sized one. BTUs measure fuel the burners can consume, not how hot or evenly the grill actually cooks.

What matters more is heat retention and even heating, which come from build quality and design. Don't choose a gas grill by its BTU number; a well-made grill with fewer BTUs often sears better and more evenly than a flimsy high-BTU one.

Build material decides how long it lasts

Grills live outdoors in rain, sun, and temperature swings, so materials determine lifespan. Stainless steel (especially higher grades) and heavy cast aluminum resist rust and last; thin painted steel is cheap and rusts out in a few seasons. Cast-iron or thick stainless cooking grates retain and transfer heat better for good sear marks (cast iron needs seasoning; stainless is lower-maintenance).

A cheap grill is often a false economy — you replace it in two or three years. Spend on solid construction and a decent cover, and a grill lasts a decade or more.

Size it by how many you cook for

Grill size is best thought of in burgers: a compact grill fits maybe 8-12 burgers (fine for a couple or small family), a mid-size does 15-20 (families and small gatherings), and a large does 25-30+ (big families and parties). Don't just buy the biggest — a huge grill wastes fuel heating empty space for everyday cooking.

Also think about burner zones: multiple independent burners let you run direct high heat on one side and indirect low heat on the other, which is how you cook different foods at once. That flexibility often matters more than sheer size.

Features worth paying for

Worthwhile: a reliable ignition (electronic push-button beats finicky igniters), a decent built-in thermometer (or room to add a probe), sturdy side tables for prep, and good grease management for easy cleanup and safety. On pellet grills, WiFi/app monitoring is genuinely useful for long smokes.

Less essential: searing side burners you'll rarely use, rotisserie kits (great if you'll actually use them, dead weight if not), and flashy lighting. Buy the features that match how you really cook, not the longest feature list.

What to skip

Don't choose a gas grill on BTUs alone — build quality and even heating matter more. Skip thin painted-steel grills that rust out fast; they're a false economy. Don't oversize 'just in case' if you mostly cook for a few. And be realistic about fuel effort — if you want food on the table in 15 minutes on a weeknight, a charcoal or pellet grill's warm-up time will frustrate you, no matter how good the flavor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Gas, charcoal, or pellet — which grill should I get?

It's about how you like to cook. Gas is fast, precise, and convenient; charcoal gives classic smoky flavor and high sear heat but takes effort; pellet grills excel at set-and-forget low-and-slow smoking but need power and sear less. Kamado ceramic grills do it all and hold heat for hours. Pick by whether speed, flavor, or smoking matters most.

Do more BTUs mean a better gas grill?

No. BTUs measure how much fuel the burners can burn, not how hot or evenly the grill cooks. A high BTU number on a big, poorly built grill can run cooler than a modest figure on a well-made one. Heat retention and even heating — which come from build quality — matter far more than the BTU number.

What grill material lasts the longest?

Higher-grade stainless steel and heavy cast aluminum resist rust and last for years outdoors, while thin painted steel rusts out in a few seasons. For grates, cast iron and thick stainless retain heat best. A well-built grill plus a cover can last a decade; a cheap one is a false economy you'll replace quickly.

What size grill do I need?

Think in burgers: a compact grill fits about 8-12 (a couple or small family), mid-size 15-20 (families and small gatherings), and large 25-30+ (parties). Don't just buy the biggest — it wastes fuel heating empty space for everyday meals. Independent burner zones for direct and indirect heat often matter more than sheer size.
