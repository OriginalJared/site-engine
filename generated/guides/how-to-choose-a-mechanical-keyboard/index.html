How to Choose a Mechanical Keyboard (Switches, Size, and Sound)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Mechanical keyboards have a jargon problem. Linears, tactiles, gaskets, hot-swap, 75% — it's a lot of vocabulary guarding a pretty simple decision. Strip it down and there are really just three things to get right: the switch feel, the size, and whether you want wireless. Nail those and you'll be happy. Here's the plain-English version.

Switches: the one choice that defines the feel

The switch under each key is what makes a mechanical keyboard feel mechanical, and there are three families. Linear switches (often called "red") are smooth top to bottom — popular for gaming and quiet-ish typing. Tactile switches ("brown") have a small bump you feel on each press — a nice middle ground that most people like for typing. Clicky switches ("blue") add a loud click — satisfying, but your coworkers and family will hate them.

If you're unsure, tactile (brown) is the safe default. If you share a space, avoid clicky. There's no "best" — it's genuinely personal, which is why the next point matters.

Hot-swap means you're not locked in

A hot-swappable keyboard lets you pull out switches and pop in different ones with no soldering. For a first keyboard, this is the feature that saves you from a costly mistake — if you buy linears and decide you want tactiles, you swap a $15 pack of switches instead of a whole keyboard.

If a board you like is hot-swap, treat it as a tiebreaker in its favor. It turns a permanent decision into a reversible one.

Size: match it to your desk and your numpad habit

Keyboards come in sizes, and the right one depends on two questions: do you use a numpad, and how much desk space do you want for your mouse? Full-size has everything including the numpad. TKL (tenkeyless) drops the numpad for more mouse room. Compact layouts (75%, 65%) shrink things further for a tidy desk.

If you crunch numbers, keep the numpad (full-size or a 96% layout that keeps it in a smaller footprint). If you game or just want space, TKL or smaller is liberating once you adjust.

Wired or wireless

Wireless mechanical keyboards are excellent now, with low latency and long battery life, and they declutter your desk. Wired keyboards never need charging and give you the absolute lowest latency, which a few competitive gamers care about.

For most people wireless is the nicer daily experience; for a pure competitive gaming setup, wired is the safe pick. Many good boards now offer both plus a 2.4GHz dongle, which is the best of both worlds.

What the extra money buys

You can get a genuinely great typing experience for $35–70 — modern budget boards include gasket mounting (for a softer, nicer-sounding typing feel) and even hot-swap. Spending more buys build quality: an aluminum case that adds heft and a premium "thock," better stabilizers under the big keys, and nicer PBT keycaps that don't go shiny.

If you just want a good keyboard, the budget tier over-delivers. If you want something that feels like a premium tool and sounds great out of the box, that's where the $150+ boards earn their keep. Skip the pure "gamer" styling markup unless you specifically want the RGB.

Frequently Asked Questions

What switch type should a beginner get?

Tactile switches (often called 'brown') are the safe default — they have a gentle bump on each keypress that most people enjoy for typing, without the loud click of 'blue' switches. If you share a room, avoid clicky switches. Linear ('red') switches are smooth and popular for gaming.

What does hot-swappable mean, and do I need it?

Hot-swap lets you change switches by pulling them out and pressing new ones in — no soldering. For a first keyboard it's worth seeking out, because it turns a permanent switch choice into a cheap, reversible one if you change your mind.

What keyboard size should I buy?

If you use a numpad, go full-size or a 96% layout (which keeps the numpad in a smaller footprint). If you game or want more mouse space, TKL (tenkeyless) or a compact 75%/65% layout frees up the desk. It comes down to your numpad habit and desk space.

How much should I spend on a mechanical keyboard?

A $35–70 board can already include gasket mounting and hot-swap for a great typing feel. Spending more ($150+) buys an aluminum case, better stabilizers, and premium PBT keycaps. The budget tier over-delivers; pay up only if you want a premium feel and sound out of the box.
