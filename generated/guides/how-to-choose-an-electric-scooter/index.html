How to Choose an Electric Scooter (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Electric scooters are a commuter tool first and a toy second, and buying one well means being honest about your actual commute — distance, hills, how you'll carry it, where you'll store it. The spec sheet leads with top speed and max range; the things that decide daily happiness are tires, brakes, and real-world range. Here's how to choose.

Range: halve the headline number

Advertised range is measured in ideal conditions — a light rider, flat ground, eco mode, moderate speed. Your real range with hills, higher speed, and a normal rider is often 50-70% of the claim. The honest spec to compare is battery capacity in watt-hours (Wh); more Wh means more real miles.

Buy for more range than your round-trip commute so you're not nursing a dying battery home, and so the battery isn't fully drained every day (which shortens its life). If the listing only shouts a mileage number with no Wh, be skeptical.

Motor power and hills

Motor wattage matters most if your route has hills. A 250-350W motor is fine for flat commutes and lighter riders; hilly routes or heavier riders want 500W or more, and steep hills benefit from dual motors. An underpowered scooter on a hill slows to a crawl and strains the motor.

Be realistic about your terrain and weight. Flat city rider? You don't need a 1,000W beast. Hilly town or a heavier rider? Don't buy the cheapest low-wattage model and expect it to climb.

Tires change everything about the ride

Pneumatic (air-filled) tires absorb bumps and grip far better than solid tires — a noticeably smoother, safer ride, especially on rough pavement. The trade-off is they can get flats. Solid tires never puncture and need zero maintenance, but they transmit every crack and can skid on wet surfaces.

For most commuters on real streets, pneumatic (or self-sealing) tires are worth it for comfort and grip. Solid tires suit riders who prize zero maintenance and ride mostly smooth surfaces. Suspension helps either way.

Brakes and safety

On something that'll hit 15-25 mph, braking is not the place to economize. Disc brakes (mechanical or hydraulic) and regenerative electronic brakes stop better and more reliably than a simple foot brake. Dual braking (front and rear) is best. Wet-weather stopping distance is longer, so good brakes matter more than top speed.

Also check for bright integrated lights and reflectors, and consider the deck width and height for stable footing. A scooter is only as good as its ability to stop when you need it to.

Portability and build

If you'll carry the scooter onto a train, up stairs, or into an office, weight and fold quality are critical. Many capable scooters weigh 30-50 lbs, which is a lot to haul far. A quick, secure folding mechanism that locks solidly (and doesn't rattle loose over time) makes the difference between convenient and annoying.

Build quality shows in the stem — a wobbly folding stem is the most common long-term complaint. Water resistance (an IP rating) matters if you'll ever ride in light rain. Prioritize a solid frame and fold over a flashy display.

What to skip

Don't trust the headline range or top speed — compare watt-hours and match motor power to your hills. Skip solid tires if you ride rough streets and value comfort. Be wary of very cheap scooters with weak brakes and flimsy folding stems; those are the parts that fail and the ones that keep you safe. And don't overbuy a heavy high-power scooter if you have a flat, short commute and have to carry it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much real range does an electric scooter have?

Usually 50-70% of the advertised figure once you factor in hills, higher speeds, and a typical rider — the claim assumes ideal conditions. Compare battery capacity in watt-hours (Wh) for a truer sense of range, and buy more than your round-trip needs so the battery isn't fully drained each day.

How much motor power do I need?

It depends on terrain and your weight. 250-350W suits flat commutes and lighter riders; 500W or more handles hills and heavier riders, with dual motors for steep climbs. An underpowered scooter crawls up hills and strains the motor, so match the wattage to your actual route rather than buying the cheapest option.

Are pneumatic or solid tires better on a scooter?

Pneumatic (air) tires give a smoother, safer ride with better grip, at the risk of flats. Solid tires never puncture and need no maintenance but transmit bumps and can skid when wet. For most commuters on real streets, pneumatic or self-sealing tires are worth it; solid tires suit smooth surfaces and zero-maintenance riders.

What brakes should an electric scooter have?

At commuter speeds, prioritize good brakes over top speed. Disc brakes (mechanical or hydraulic) plus regenerative electronic braking stop more reliably than a basic foot brake, and dual front-and-rear braking is best. Wet conditions lengthen stopping distance, so strong, dependable brakes are a core safety feature, not an upgrade.
