How to Choose an Office Chair (Without Overpaying)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Most people buy an office chair the way they buy a mattress: too fast, based on a ten-second sit-test that tells them almost nothing about hour eight of a workday. The chair that feels great in the store can wreck your lower back by Thursday. Here's what actually matters when you're sitting in one full-time, and what you can ignore.

Start with seat depth

This is the part nobody markets, and it's the first thing to check. If the front edge of the seat digs into the back of your knees, no amount of lumbar support will save you.

You want two or three fingers of space between the seat edge and the back of your knees. A good chair lets you slide the seat forward and back to dial that in. A cheap one makes you live with whatever depth it shipped with, which is fine if it happens to fit your legs and a problem if it doesn't.

Adjustable lumbar beats a fixed bulge

Every listing brags about lumbar support. The word to look for is adjustable — height and firmness — not a fixed curve that may or may not line up with your actual spine.

A built-in adjustable system beats a strap-on pillow that slides out of place every time you stand up. If you find a chair under $250 with real adjustable lumbar, put it on your shortlist. That one feature separates a chair that supports you from one that just looks like it does.

Don't overlook the armrests

Your shoulders pay for bad armrests. Too high and you shrug all day; too low and you lean. At a minimum you want arms that move up and down.

The pricier chairs move in four directions, which is genuinely useful if you bounce between a keyboard, a tablet, and a phone all day. If you mostly type, height-adjustable arms are plenty. Four-way arms are a nice-to-have, not a must.

What to actually spend

Under about $150, you're buying "better than a dining chair" — fine for a couple of hours, not for full-time work. The $200 to $400 range is the sweet spot for most people: real adjustability, mesh that breathes, a frame that won't start wobbling in a year.

Above that, you're mostly paying for longevity and resale, not extra comfort. A Steelcase or Herman Miller will outlast three cheap chairs and holds its value if you ever sell it. That's a real argument if you sit forty hours a week in it, and a waste of money if you don't.

Mesh or foam?

It comes down to one question: do you run hot? Mesh breathes and keeps its shape for years. Foam feels cushier on day one and warmer on a July afternoon, and it flattens over time.

Neither is better in the abstract. Pick for your body and your room. If your office gets warm or you tend to overheat, go mesh. If you want a softer seat and temperature isn't an issue, foam is fine.

What you can ignore

A few things get hyped more than they deserve. Headrests are nice if you lean back and recline a lot, and pointless if you sit upright and work. "Gaming" styling is usually a foam chair with racing stripes and a markup. Weight-capacity numbers far above your actual weight don't make a chair more comfortable.

And be a little skeptical of a chair with thousands of five-star reviews but no real adjustments — people often rate the fast delivery and low price, not how their back feels six months in.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I spend on an office chair?

For full-time work, $200 to $400 hits the sweet spot — you get real adjustability and a frame that lasts. Under $150 is okay for light, occasional use. Premium chairs above $1,000 mainly buy you longevity and resale value, which pays off only if you sit in it most of the day.

Is mesh or foam better for an office chair?

Neither is universally better. Mesh breathes and keeps its shape, so it's the pick if you run hot or sit for long stretches. Foam is softer initially but warmer and tends to flatten over time. Choose based on whether you overheat and how soft you like the seat.

What's the most important feature in an office chair?

Adjustability, and specifically seat depth and adjustable lumbar. A chair you can tune to your body will beat a plusher one you can't. If you have to pick one thing to get right, make it a seat that fits your leg length and lumbar support you can actually position.

Are expensive chairs like Herman Miller worth it?

If you sit full-time, often yes — they outlast several cheap chairs, stay comfortable for years, and hold resale value. If you only use a desk a few hours a day, a good $250 to $400 chair gives you most of the benefit for a fraction of the price.
