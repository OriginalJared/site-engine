How to Choose a Projector (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Projectors are the category where cheap listings lie the most — fake resolutions, invented brightness numbers, and 'native 1080p' claims that aren't. The single most important factor is also the one buyers overlook: brightness, matched to your room. Get that wrong and even an expensive projector looks washed out. Here's how to read past the fiction and buy one that actually looks good.

Brightness is king — and matched to your room

Brightness (measured in lumens) matters more than any other spec, because a projector competes with ambient light. A dim projector looks stunning in a pitch-black room and like a faded ghost with the lights on. Be realistic about where you'll use it: a dedicated dark home theater can use fewer lumens; a living room with windows or any ambient light needs a lot more.

Be skeptical of lumen claims on cheap projectors — many quote inflated 'LED lumens' or made-up figures. Look for ANSI lumens, the honest standard, and buy more brightness than you think you need if the room isn't dark.

Real vs. fake 4K and resolution

This is the biggest spec fraud in the category. Cheap projectors advertise '4K' or '1080p supported' when the native panel is actually 720p or lower — they just accept a higher-resolution signal and downscale it. 'Supports 4K' is not the same as 'is 4K.'

Look for the native resolution, which is the real panel. Native 1080p is the sweet spot for most buyers; true 4K projectors exist but cost much more (and some use pixel-shifting to simulate 4K from a 1080p chip, which is fine but worth knowing). If a sub-$200 projector claims 4K native, it's lying.

Throw distance and placement

Throw ratio determines how big an image you get at a given distance — and whether the projector even fits your room. A standard projector needs several feet to fill a big screen; a short-throw sits close; an ultra-short-throw (UST) sits inches from the wall. Measure your room and where the projector can go before buying.

Also check for lens shift and zoom (for flexible placement) versus relying on keystone correction, which squares the image digitally but slightly degrades it. A projector that physically fits and aligns to your wall beats one you have to fight with keystone to use.

Light source: lamp, LED, or laser

Traditional lamp projectors are bright and cheaper up front but the bulb dims over time and needs replacing (an ongoing cost every few thousand hours). LED projectors last far longer (tens of thousands of hours), run cooler, and never need a bulb, but tend to be dimmer. Laser projectors are bright, long-lived, and fast to start — the premium option.

For occasional use, a lamp projector is fine if you accept eventual bulb replacement. For frequent use or set-and-forget longevity, LED or laser saves hassle and the recurring bulb cost. Factor the bulb into a lamp model's true price.

Smart features, sound, and inputs

Many projectors now include built-in streaming apps, which is convenient but often laggy or missing key apps — a cheap external streaming stick usually works better, so don't pay a big premium for built-in smarts. Built-in speakers are universally weak; plan to use external speakers or a soundbar for anything beyond casual viewing.

Check inputs: enough HDMI ports for your devices, and HDMI eARC if you'll connect a soundbar. For gaming, look at input lag. These practical details matter more day to day than a long smart-feature list.

What to skip

Ignore inflated 'LED lumen' and made-up brightness numbers — look for ANSI lumens and buy enough for your room's light. Don't trust '4K' or '1080p' claims without checking native resolution; sub-$200 '4K' projectors are not 4K. Don't lean on keystone correction as a substitute for proper placement. And don't pay extra for built-in streaming or speakers you'll replace with a $40 stick and real speakers anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

What matters more, projector brightness or resolution?

Brightness, in most cases. A projector competes with ambient light, so a dim unit looks washed out with any lights on, no matter its resolution. Match brightness (in honest ANSI lumens) to your room — a dark theater needs less, a living room with windows needs much more — and treat it as the top priority.

Is a cheap '4K' projector really 4K?

Usually not. Many budget projectors advertise '4K' or '1080p' based on the signal they accept, while their actual native panel is 720p or lower and downscales the image. Look for the native resolution — that's the real panel. Native 1080p is the sweet spot for most buyers; true 4K costs significantly more.

How do I know a projector will fit my room?

Check the throw ratio against your room's dimensions. Standard projectors need several feet to fill a screen, short-throw sit closer, and ultra-short-throw sit inches from the wall. Measure the distance available and favor models with lens shift and optical zoom for placement flexibility rather than relying on image-degrading keystone correction.

Lamp, LED, or laser — which light source is best?

Lamp projectors are bright and cheap up front but the bulb dims and needs periodic replacement. LED lasts tens of thousands of hours and runs cool but is often dimmer. Laser is bright, long-lived, and premium-priced. For occasional use, lamp is fine if you accept bulb costs; for frequent or set-and-forget use, LED or laser saves hassle.
