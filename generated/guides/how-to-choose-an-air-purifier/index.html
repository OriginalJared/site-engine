How to Choose an Air Purifier (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Air purifiers are simple machines with a confusing sales pitch. Strip away the wellness language and only three things really matter: does it move enough clean air for your room, does it use a real filter, and what will replacement filters cost you over a few years. Nail those and the rest is noise. Here's how to buy one that actually clears a room.

CADR and room size are the whole decision

CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) tells you how much clean air the purifier produces, and it's the one number worth comparing. It needs to match your room: a purifier rated for a 300-square-foot room will barely touch a 600-square-foot open-plan space.

A useful rule is to buy for a bigger room than you have, so the unit can clean the air several times an hour on a lower, quieter setting instead of screaming at max. Match CADR to your actual square footage first; everything else is secondary.

Insist on true HEPA

The filter is the heart of the machine. 'True HEPA' (or H13 HEPA) captures 99.97% of fine particles and is what you want for dust, pollen, smoke, and pet dander. Be wary of vaguer phrases like 'HEPA-type' or 'HEPA-style,' which are weaker filters hiding behind the name.

Many purifiers add an activated carbon layer for odors and gases — worth having if you deal with cooking smells, smoke, or VOCs. If smells are your main concern, make sure there's a real carbon filter with meaningful weight to it, not a token mesh.

The filter cost nobody mentions

The sticker price is only half the story. Replacement filters are an ongoing cost, and some cheap purifiers use expensive proprietary filters that make the machine cost more over three years than a pricier unit with affordable filters.

Before buying, look up the replacement filter price and how often it's needed (typically every 6-12 months). A purifier is a subscription in disguise — do that math up front so you're not surprised later.

Noise, because you'll run it constantly

An air purifier only works when it's on, and you'll want it running in bedrooms and offices where noise matters. Check the decibel rating at the lowest and highest speeds. A unit that's whisper-quiet on low but a jet engine on high is fine — as long as its low or medium setting still cleans your room adequately (another reason to oversize on CADR).

A quiet sleep mode and a dimmable display are small things that make a big difference in a bedroom.

Features worth having vs. filler

A genuinely useful feature is an auto mode with a real particle sensor — it ramps up when air quality drops and idles when it's clean, so you don't manage it manually. An air-quality readout that shows current conditions is satisfying and informative.

Less essential: app control and voice assistants are nice-to-haves, not reasons to buy. Ignore 'ionizers' as a selling point — some produce trace ozone, and the HEPA filter is doing the real work regardless.

What to skip

Skip anything leaning on UV or ionizer claims as the main feature; the filter matters far more, and ionizers can produce small amounts of ozone. Avoid 'HEPA-type' filters — pay for true HEPA. And don't under-size to save money: a purifier that's too small for the room runs loud and never quite clears the air, which is the most common reason people give up on them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size air purifier do I need for my room?

Match the purifier's CADR rating to your room's square footage, and ideally oversize a little. A unit rated for a larger room can clean your air several times an hour on a lower, quieter setting, which is both more effective and more pleasant than running a too-small unit at max.

Is 'True HEPA' different from 'HEPA-type'?

Yes, importantly. True HEPA (often H13) captures 99.97% of fine particles and is what you want. 'HEPA-type' or 'HEPA-style' are weaker filters trading on the name. For dust, pollen, smoke, and pet dander, insist on true HEPA, ideally with an activated carbon layer for odors.

How much do replacement filters cost?

It varies a lot, and it's the hidden cost of the category. Filters typically need replacing every 6-12 months, and some budget purifiers use pricey proprietary filters. Always check the replacement price before buying — a cheap unit with expensive filters can cost more over a few years than a pricier one.

Do I need the app and smart features?

Not really. The feature worth having is an auto mode with a real particle sensor that adjusts the fan to air quality. App control and voice assistants are conveniences, not reasons to choose one purifier over another with better CADR, a true HEPA filter, and cheaper replacement filters.
