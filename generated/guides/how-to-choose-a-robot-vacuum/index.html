How to Choose a Robot Vacuum (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

A robot vacuum is one of those purchases where the spec sheet lies to you. The number manufacturers scream the loudest about — suction in Pascals — is the one that matters least once you're past a certain point. What actually decides whether you keep using the thing is how well it gets around your home and how little you have to babysit it. Here's what to look at.

Navigation is the whole ballgame

This is the single biggest divider between a robot you love and one you return. Cheaper robots bounce around semi-randomly and miss spots; good ones use LiDAR to map your home and clean in tidy, logical rows.

LiDAR mapping also unlocks the features you'll actually use day to day: no-go zones so it stops eating the cat's water bowl, room-by-room cleaning, and the ability to send it to just the kitchen after dinner. If a listing doesn't clearly say LiDAR (or at least a real mapping system), assume it's a bump-and-roll model and keep looking unless you're on the tightest budget.

Suction: enough is enough

Every brand advertises a Pascal (Pa) number, and bigger sounds better. In reality, anything from about 5,000Pa up handles everyday dust, crumbs, and hardwood just fine. The jump to 10,000 or 20,000Pa mostly matters if you have thick carpet or a lot of pet hair ground into it.

Don't pay a big premium chasing the highest number. Past a point it just means more noise and faster battery drain for cleaning you won't notice.

The dock is where the magic (or the chore) lives

A self-emptying dock is the upgrade people rave about. Instead of emptying a tiny onboard bin every couple of runs, the robot empties itself into a larger bin in the dock that you touch maybe once a month. For most people this is the difference between "set it and forget it" and "another thing to maintain."

The fancier docks also wash and dry the mop pads with hot water. Great if you mop a lot; overkill if you don't. Just know the trade-off: these docks are big and need real floor space.

Mopping: useful, but manage expectations

Most mid-range and up robots now mop as well as vacuum. It's genuinely handy for daily dust and light footprints on hard floors. It is not going to scrub a dried spaghetti-sauce spill — that's still on you.

If you have a mix of rugs and hard floor, look for a model that lifts its mop pads over carpet (so it doesn't drag a wet pad across your rug). That one feature separates the good hybrids from the annoying ones.

If you have pets, read this part

Pet hair is where cheap robots go to die — it wraps around the brush until the thing is basically dragging a wig. Look for an anti-tangle or rubber roller brush specifically designed to shed hair, and AI obstacle avoidance if you'd rather it steer around a mess than smear it across the house.

Pet owners are also the people who benefit most from strong suction and a self-emptying dock, since hair fills a small bin fast.

What to skip

Ignore marketing around "AI" that doesn't describe a concrete feature. Be skeptical of a cheap robot with a huge suction number and no mapping — that combination almost always means a bouncer with a loud motor. And don't over-buy a hot-water-washing mega-dock if you live in a small apartment with mostly carpet; you're paying for a feature you won't use and giving up floor space for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need LiDAR navigation in a robot vacuum?

For most homes, yes. LiDAR mapping is what lets the robot clean in efficient rows instead of bouncing randomly, and it enables no-go zones and room-specific cleaning. Bump-and-roll models are only worth it at the very bottom of the budget range or in a single small room.

How much suction (Pa) does a robot vacuum really need?

Around 5,000Pa and up handles everyday dust, crumbs, and hard floors well. Higher numbers (10,000–20,000Pa) mainly help with thick carpet and ground-in pet hair. Don't pay a big premium just to chase the largest figure.

Is a self-emptying dock worth it?

For most people, yes — it's the feature that turns a robot vacuum into something you actually forget about. Instead of emptying a small bin every couple of runs, you empty a larger dock bin roughly once a month. The main downside is the dock's size.

Can a robot vacuum replace a regular vacuum?

For day-to-day maintenance on hard floors and low-pile carpet, largely yes. For deep-cleaning thick carpet, stairs, and upholstery, you'll still want a traditional or stick vacuum. Think of a robot as the thing that keeps the floor tidy between deeper cleans.
