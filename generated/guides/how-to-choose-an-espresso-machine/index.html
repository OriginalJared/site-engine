How to Choose an Espresso Machine (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

Buying an espresso machine is really about answering one question honestly: how much do you want to be involved? The category runs from push-a-button to full barista ritual, and a mismatch there is why expensive machines end up gathering dust. There's also a secret: the grinder matters as much as the machine. Here's how to choose the right level and the specs that back it up.

Pick your machine type by effort level

There are four broad types. Super-automatics do everything at a button press — grind, dose, brew, sometimes froth — for people who want café coffee with zero fuss and will pay for it. Automatic and semi-automatic machines let you control the shot while handling pressure automatically — the sweet spot for people who want to learn without going fully manual. Manual lever machines are for enthusiasts who enjoy the ritual.

Capsule/pod machines are the simplest and cheapest but limit you to pods. Decide how hands-on you want to be first; it rules out most of the catalog instantly.

The grinder matters as much as the machine

This is the thing beginners don't know: espresso quality lives and dies by the grind, and pre-ground coffee makes bad espresso no matter how good the machine. You need a burr grinder capable of fine, consistent, adjustable espresso grinds.

So your real budget is machine plus grinder. A machine with a built-in grinder solves this in one box (convenient, but harder to upgrade). A separate quality grinder costs more up front but often grinds better and lasts across machine upgrades. Either way, don't buy a nice machine and feed it supermarket pre-ground.

Pressure, temperature, and why they matter

Espresso needs about 9 bars of brewing pressure — but beware machines bragging about '15 bars' or '20 bars' as if more is better. That's the pump's max, not what's at the coffee; nearly every machine can hit the 9 bars that matter, so a big bar number is marketing.

What genuinely matters is temperature stability. Espresso is fussy about heat, and machines with PID temperature control hold a steady, repeatable temperature shot to shot. On higher-end machines, that's a feature worth seeking; cheap machines swing in temperature and produce inconsistent results.

Steam power if you drink milk drinks

If you mostly drink lattes and cappuccinos, the steam wand matters as much as the shot. A proper steam wand lets you texture microfoam like a café; weak steamers on cheap machines produce big, soapy bubbles instead of silky milk. Single-boiler machines make you wait between brewing and steaming; dual-boiler or heat-exchanger machines do both at once.

If you're an espresso-only or americano drinker, steaming barely matters and you can save money. If milk drinks are your daily, prioritize steam performance — it's where cheap machines disappoint most.

Portafilter and build basics

A 'commercial-style' 58mm portafilter (the handle that holds the coffee) is a good sign — it's the standard size, so accessories and tampers are easy to find, and it usually indicates a more serious machine. Pressurized portafilters (common on entry machines) are forgiving for beginners but cap quality; non-pressurized reward good technique.

Metal internals and a decent build last; all-plastic machines at rock-bottom prices tend to fail. Also check the water reservoir size and whether it's easy to refill, since you'll do it constantly.

What to skip

Ignore big '15/20 bar' pressure claims — 9 bars is what matters and almost everything hits it. Don't buy a quality machine and pair it with pre-ground coffee or a cheap blade grinder; you'll waste the machine's potential. Skip super-automatic money if you actually enjoy the process, and skip a complex manual machine if you just want coffee fast. And don't over-invest in steaming if you drink your espresso black.

Frequently Asked Questions

What type of espresso machine should a beginner buy?

Decide by how hands-on you want to be. Super-automatics do everything at a button press for maximum convenience; semi-automatics let you control the shot while automating pressure, which is the popular learning sweet spot; pod machines are simplest but limit you to capsules. Most beginners who want to learn are happiest with a semi-automatic plus a good grinder.

Do I really need a separate grinder?

For good espresso, yes. Espresso quality depends heavily on a fine, consistent, adjustable grind that pre-ground coffee and blade grinders can't provide. Budget for a burr grinder along with the machine, or buy a machine with a built-in burr grinder. Pairing a nice machine with supermarket pre-ground wastes its potential.

Does a higher bar pressure make better espresso?

No. Espresso is brewed at about 9 bars, and the '15 bar' or '20 bar' numbers on boxes are the pump's maximum, not the pressure at the coffee — nearly every machine reaches the 9 bars that matter. It's a marketing figure. Temperature stability (such as PID control) matters far more for shot quality.

What matters most if I mostly drink lattes?

The steam wand. Texturing silky microfoam needs a capable steamer; weak steamers on cheap machines make big soapy bubbles. Dual-boiler or heat-exchanger machines let you brew and steam without waiting. If milk drinks are your daily habit, prioritize steam performance over almost everything else.
