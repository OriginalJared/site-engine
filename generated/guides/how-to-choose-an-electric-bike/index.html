How to Choose an Electric Bike (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

E-bikes are the rare purchase where the marketing numbers (top speed, max range, peak watts) are both the loudest and the least trustworthy. What actually determines whether you love the bike is how the motor delivers power, whether the range holds up in the real world, and whether it fits your body and the law where you ride. Here's how to see past the spec sheet.

Classes and the law come first

In the US, e-bikes are grouped into three classes: Class 1 (pedal-assist up to 20 mph), Class 2 (adds a throttle, 20 mph), and Class 3 (pedal-assist up to 28 mph). This isn't trivia — it determines where you can legally ride. Many bike paths and trails ban Class 3 or throttles entirely.

Before anything else, check the rules for where you'll actually ride (city, trails, campus) and buy a class that's allowed there. A fast Class 3 bike you can't legally take on your local path is the wrong bike no matter how good it is.

Hub motor vs. mid-drive

Hub motors (in the wheel) are cheaper, quieter, and nearly maintenance-free — great for flat-to-rolling commutes and most casual riders. Mid-drive motors (at the pedals) drive the chain, so they climb hills far better and feel more like a natural bike, but they cost more and wear the chain faster.

If you face serious hills or want the most bike-like ride, a mid-drive is worth it. For flat city commuting and value, a quality hub motor is all most people need. Don't pay mid-drive money for terrain that doesn't demand it.

Range claims are best-case fiction

The advertised range assumes a light rider on flat ground in the lowest assist level pedaling hard — conditions you'll rarely meet. Real-world range on higher assist, with hills, wind, and a normal rider, is often half the number on the box.

What actually matters is battery capacity in watt-hours (Wh). More Wh means more real range. If range matters, compare watt-hours between bikes rather than trusting the headline mile claim, and assume you'll get meaningfully less than advertised.

Torque sensor vs. cadence sensor

This is the feel difference most buyers never hear about but immediately notice. A cadence sensor just detects that you're pedaling and dumps in assist — it can feel like an on/off switch, surging then coasting. A torque sensor measures how hard you pedal and scales assist to match, so it feels smooth, natural, and responsive, like a strong tailwind.

Torque sensors cost more and are the mark of a nicer bike. If you can, prioritize one — it's the single biggest factor in how enjoyable the bike is to ride.

Fit, weight, and practicality

An e-bike is still a bike, so frame size and style matter. Step-through frames are easier to mount, especially for shorter riders or anyone with mobility concerns; step-over frames are stiffer and sportier. Get the size right for your height, as you would with any bike.

Weight is the hidden catch: e-bikes are heavy (often 50-70 lbs), which matters if you'll carry it upstairs or lift it onto a rack. Folding models help with storage and transport. Think about where it'll live and how it'll move when the battery's dead.

What to skip

Don't trust the headline range or top-speed numbers — compare watt-hours and buy a legal class for your area instead. Don't overpay for a mid-drive if you ride flat ground. Be cautious of ultra-cheap e-bikes with no-name batteries and poor support — the battery is the most expensive, safety-critical part, and a bad one is dangerous and costly to replace. Buy from brands with real warranty and service.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between e-bike Class 1, 2, and 3?

Class 1 is pedal-assist up to 20 mph, Class 2 adds a throttle (also 20 mph), and Class 3 is pedal-assist up to 28 mph. The class determines where you can legally ride — many trails and paths restrict Class 3 or throttles. Check your local rules and buy a class that's allowed where you'll ride.

Hub motor or mid-drive — which should I get?

Hub motors are cheaper, quieter, and low-maintenance, and they're great for flat commuting and casual riding. Mid-drive motors climb hills much better and feel more natural but cost more and wear the chain faster. Choose mid-drive for serious hills or a bike-like ride; a hub motor for flat terrain and value.

How accurate are e-bike range claims?

Not very. Advertised range assumes ideal conditions — light rider, flat ground, lowest assist — and real-world range is often half that with hills, wind, and higher assist. Compare battery capacity in watt-hours (Wh) rather than the headline mile figure, and expect meaningfully less range than the box claims.

Does a torque sensor really matter on an e-bike?

Yes — it's the biggest factor in ride feel. A torque sensor scales assist to how hard you pedal, feeling smooth and natural, while a cheaper cadence sensor dumps assist on or off and can feel surgy. If enjoyable, responsive riding matters to you, prioritize a bike with a torque sensor.
