How to Choose a 4K Monitor (What Actually Matters)

By the Halcott Magazine editorial team · Updated November 2026

4K monitors are where marketing gets loudest and least honest. Boxes shout resolution, a huge refresh number, and 'HDR' in big letters — but two monitors with identical headline specs can look worlds apart. What actually matters is the panel, how bright it really gets, and whether the ports match the machine you're plugging in. Here's how to cut through it.

Panel type sets the ceiling

Almost every quality 4K monitor today is IPS, VA, or OLED, and that choice decides more about the picture than any number on the box. IPS gives you accurate color and wide viewing angles — the safe default for mixed work and media. VA trades some viewing angle for much deeper contrast, which looks great for movies and dark scenes but can smear a little in fast motion.

OLED is the new premium tier: perfect blacks, dazzling contrast, and instant response. It's gorgeous, but it costs more and carries a small risk of burn-in if you leave static elements on screen all day. If you do heavy office work with fixed taskbars and toolbars, a good IPS is the lower-stress choice.

Refresh rate: match it to what you do

A 4K monitor at 60Hz is perfectly fine for work, photo, and video. You only need 120Hz or 144Hz if you game or just want a noticeably smoother desktop — and pushing 4K at those rates takes a seriously powerful graphics card plus the right cable.

Don't pay for a 144Hz 4K panel if your computer can't drive it; you'll be stuck at 60Hz anyway. For most people buying a 4K monitor primarily for productivity, spend the money on color and brightness instead of chasing refresh rate.

HDR is mostly a sticker — check the real numbers

This is the biggest trap in the category. 'HDR10 supported' means almost nothing; a monitor can accept an HDR signal and still display it badly. What matters is peak brightness and local dimming. 'DisplayHDR 400' is essentially regular brightness with an HDR label — fine, but don't expect a real HDR experience.

For HDR that actually looks like something, you want DisplayHDR 600 or higher with real local dimming zones, or an OLED. If HDR matters to you, look past the logo and find the nits rating and dimming-zone count.

Size, and why pixel density matters

At 4K, 27 inches is razor-sharp but text can be tiny until you scale it; 32 inches gives you more usable space with comfortable sharpness; 40-plus-inch ultrawides are a desk-filling productivity play. The thing to understand is that 4K on a 27-inch screen is much denser than 4K on a 43-inch screen.

Bigger is not automatically better here — a 32-inch 4K hits a sweet spot for most desks. Measure your space and sit distance before you buy a 42-inch panel you'll be craning your neck across.

Ports decide whether it just works

Check that the monitor's inputs match your device before anything else. Most machines use HDMI 2.0, HDMI 2.1, or DisplayPort, and only HDMI 2.1 and DisplayPort 1.4+ can do 4K at high refresh rates. If you have a laptop, a USB-C port that carries video and charges the laptop over one cable is a genuine quality-of-life upgrade.

Also look at how many inputs there are and whether there's a built-in USB hub. A monitor that charges your laptop and acts as a dock turns a cable mess into a single plug.

What to skip

Ignore 'HDR' claims with no brightness number behind them, and don't chase a 144Hz 4K panel unless you own the graphics card to feed it. Built-in speakers are universally mediocre — don't let them sway the decision. And be wary of ultra-cheap 4K monitors with vague panel descriptions; a no-name 4K at a suspiciously low price is usually a dim, slow VA panel with poor color.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 4K worth it over 1440p for a monitor?

For productivity, photo, and video work, yes — the extra sharpness and desktop space are real. For gaming, 1440p is often the smarter buy because it's far easier to drive at high frame rates. If you mostly work and watch media, 4K; if you mostly game competitively, strong 1440p.

Do I need HDMI 2.1 for a 4K monitor?

Only if you want 4K above 60Hz. HDMI 2.0 and DisplayPort 1.4 handle 4K at 60Hz fine for work. For 4K at 120Hz or 144Hz gaming, you need HDMI 2.1 or DisplayPort 1.4 with DSC, plus a graphics card that can actually push those frames.

What size 4K monitor should I get?

27 inches is extremely sharp but may need display scaling for readable text; 32 inches is the comfortable sweet spot for most desks; 40-plus inches is for people who want one giant workspace. Match the size to your desk depth and how far you sit.

Is the HDR on budget 4K monitors any good?

Usually not. 'DisplayHDR 400' and 'HDR10 supported' labels often mean the monitor accepts an HDR signal without the brightness or local dimming to display it well. For real HDR, look for DisplayHDR 600+ with local dimming, or an OLED panel.
